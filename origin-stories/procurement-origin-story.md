# The Origin Story of Procurement
## From buying goods to creating enterprise value
SuccessLabs Academy | Origin Story Sprint | PROC-OS-001

## The opening scene
A maintenance engineer needs a replacement pump before a production line stops. The buyer finds three prices. Finance asks whether money is available. Operations asks whether the pump will arrive in time. The supplier asks whether the specification is correct.

One request exposes an entire enterprise: people, decisions, data, contracts, technology and trust. Procurement begins with a need. Its real outcome is the capability to keep a promise.

This is a fictional teaching scenario, not a reported client incident.

## The path up the mountain
The following stages are a teaching lens, not a universal chronology. Organisations can operate across several stages at once.

### 1. The foothills: buying what is needed
Start with a request, a supplier and a purchase. Paper forms, phone calls and local relationships make the work visible to the people involved but difficult for others to trace.
Question: How do we get the right item, at the right time, for an acceptable price?
Learner experiment: handle the pump request using only separate messages. Record missing information and repeated questions.

### 2. The first climb: controlling commitments
Approvals, purchase orders, receiving records and invoice checks introduce accountability. Control improves, but unnecessary handoffs can delay urgent work.
Question: Who can commit the organisation, and what evidence justifies payment?
Learner experiment: separate requester, approver, buyer and receiver responsibilities; define an emergency route with retrospective review.

### 3. The connected trail: linking the enterprise
A shared process connects demand, budget, supplier data, orders, receipts, invoices and payment. Poor master data can still create errors at scale.
Question: Can each participant trust the same facts?
Learner experiment: trace one purchase from request to payment and identify its system of record at each step.

### 4. The wider view: sourcing for value
Supplier selection considers service, quality, reliability, operating cost and contractual obligations alongside purchase price.
Question: What will this decision cost and enable over the full life of the asset?
Learner experiment: compare a low-price pump with a more reliable alternative using explicit assumptions for maintenance and downtime.

### 5. The exposed ridge: resilience and responsibility
A supplier failure reveals dependencies beyond a single purchase. Alternative sources, risk assessment and evidence of responsible supply become part of design.
Question: What happens when our preferred supplier cannot deliver?
Learner experiment: simulate a four-week delay. Rework the sourcing decision and explain the trade-off between cost and continuity.

### 6. The horizon: assisted and bounded autonomous decisions
Future scenario through 2050: digital assistants could help clarify demand, prepare sourcing documents and route routine transactions within approved limits. This is an exploration, not a prediction or a claim about current product capabilities.
Question: Which decisions can be delegated, under what limits, with whose accountability?
Learner experiment: define spending limits, approved supplier rules, escalation triggers, audit evidence and a stop mechanism. Retain accountable human review for exceptions and material commitments.

## The process learners must understand
1. Clarify the business need and specification.
2. Check demand, available stock and budget.
3. Assess suppliers and source competitively where appropriate.
4. Evaluate total value and risk; document the award.
5. Agree terms and establish the purchase commitment.
6. Receive goods or confirm service delivery.
7. Validate the invoice against applicable order and receipt evidence; resolve exceptions.
8. Authorise payment through finance controls.
9. Review supplier performance and feed lessons into the next decision.

Source-to-contract establishes suppliers and agreements. Procure-to-pay executes purchasing and settlement. Together they connect strategic choices with operational evidence.

## A 120-minute Origin Story Sprint
- 0–10 minutes: tell the pump story; predict where it will fail.
- 10–25 minutes: reconstruct the six stages; compare with learners' own experience.
- 25–45 minutes: map the purchase, roles, handoffs and evidence.
- 45–65 minutes: compare two suppliers using total value and explicit assumptions.
- 65–85 minutes: introduce a supplier disruption; redesign the decision.
- 85–105 minutes: design a future assistant with delegation limits and escalation.
- 105–120 minutes: demonstrate the process and defend one trade-off.

## Evidence of transformation
Each learner submits one process map, one supplier decision sheet, one exception path and one five-minute architecture explanation. Peer review checks whether every step has an owner, required data, a decision rule and evidence of completion.

Use a simulated baseline: count handoffs, missing fields and clarification rounds in the first run. Repeat after redesign. Report the measured change without presenting simulation results as production savings.

## Twelve architecture lenses
- Enterprise Architect: connect procurement outcomes to enterprise priorities.
- Business Architect: define capabilities, value streams, roles and decision rights.
- Integration Architect: connect supplier, purchasing, inventory and finance events.
- Domain Architect: clarify procurement terminology and boundaries.
- Cloud & Infrastructure Architect: design availability, recovery and connectivity.
- Application & Process Architect: align workflow and application responsibilities.
- AI Architect: design assistance, evaluation, delegation limits and human review.
- Security Architect: protect supplier and bank data; separate conflicting duties.
- Industry Architect: adapt sourcing and assurance to sector-specific needs.
- Data Architect: govern supplier, material, contract and transaction data.
- UI/UX Architect: make requests and exceptions understandable and accessible.
- Technology Architect: assess technical fit, interoperability and lifecycle needs.
